How would you know if it was wrong?

How to run the evals session so that every idea arrives after the room has already felt the problem it solves. One running example, one question asked four times, a guess locked before every reveal.

90 minutes in a room, 60 if tightWorks with 8 to 80 peopleStore support agent, order 4821, throughoutUpdated 1 Nov 2026

The one rule of the session

Never say a word before the room has done the thing it names. Not "eval", not "rubric", not "judge". The room taps the wrong word, then you say "grader". The room writes a question down, then you say "failure mode". If you catch yourself defining something, stop and make them do it first.

Why four steps

The earlier ten-module version taught the same material as a syllabus. This version is built the way Feynman taught: something you can see, a question, a prediction you commit to, an experiment, and the principle that falls out. The whole session installs one habit, the question in the title, and each step asks it one level up.

Before the room arrives

  • Open the lab yourself the night before at 100x-curriculum.vercel.app/c8/evals-lab and run all four steps with "Use the recorded run". About 35 minutes alone. You need to have felt every reveal to time them.
  • Have a Groq key in the browser you will project. Students have one from Lecture 5. The live judge makes step 3 land; the recorded run is the fallback and reproduces the numbers below exactly.
  • Projector shows the lab, not slides. The lab is the deck. Keep the notes page open in a second tab for the research numbers when someone asks for a source.
  • Everyone opens the lab on their own laptop too. The guesses are theirs. Phones work for steps 1 and 3; the rubric in step 2 wants a keyboard.
  • A whiteboard with four empty columns headed Look, Write it down, Check the checker, Change one thing. You fill each column with one sentence at the end of its step, in the room's words.
  • The lab is one point per screen. Thirty-four screens across the four steps, the first one the setup. The headline of each screen is the instruction (a coral dot marks it), the framed box under it is the one thing to look at or do, and the Continue button does not exist until that thing is done. Enter or the right arrow is Continue; number keys pick options. On a laptop or projector a sketch sits beside every screen and changes as the room acts: point at it when you say the line. Phones show the screen alone. Do not skip ahead on the projector; the room's laptops are gated the same way.
  • Clear the lab state if you reuse a machine. Run localStorage.removeItem('c8.evalslab.v3') in the console.

Each step in the room

Step 1 · 15 minutes

Look

Is it wrong?

What the room does

  • Taps the wrong word on the projected trace. Someone finds Thursday in under ten seconds. Let the silence sit, then point at the PASS stamp.
  • Taps the card that says what happened on the refund trace. The state card, not the answer.
  • Locks a guess: how many of thirty are wrong. Hands up per bucket.
  • Reads five traces and types one sentence each, or presses "Nothing wrong". Then sees all thirty with the instructor's line.

What you say, and when

After Thursday: "You just did what the grader could not. The grader is a model. A model on top of a model is not code."
After the refund card: "Grade the database, not the sentence." Write it in column 1.
After 18 of 30: "Your eyes work. They will not read thirty rows after every prompt change, and the next person will not read them the way you did."

Watch for

  • People typing a category ("hallucination") instead of a sentence. Ask them to say it out loud and type that.
  • Row 5 ("about a dozen") passed by half the room. Point at the empty log. Whole trace, not the answer.
  • Row 14 is a pass. "Nothing wrong" is a finding; they need passes in step 3.

Step 2 · 30 minutes

Write the question down

What would you check, every time?

What the room does

  • Names six pre-sorted piles as yes-or-no questions, one pile per screen. Compares with the instructor's six.
  • Locks a guess: how many could code answer.
  • Picks code or a model, one pile per screen. Picking code runs the check on thirty rows at once and shows the count.
  • Writes the judge's one question for made-up detail, with PASS, FAIL plus quote, UNKNOWN. Locks it.
  • Locks a guess on row 2, then runs the judge. Twenty cards land face down.

What you say, and when

On the scale card: "Three graders, three numbers. One question, three yeses. A scale hides disagreement."
After five of six: "You expected a session about judge prompts. It is mostly about assertions." Column 2: code first.
Before they lock: "Once you have seen the outputs, every edit to this is a thumb on the scale. Lock it now."

Watch for

  • Names that are not questions ("hallucinations"). Ask: would two colleagues answer it the same way?
  • People choosing a model for "wrong number". Ask what a sheet formula would need to see. A number against the tool result.
  • The "Use the masterclass rubric" button. Fine for anyone stuck after three minutes; the point is the lock, not the prose.
  • Groq rate limits with eighty people running at once. Stagger: odd rows run live, even rows use the recorded run.

Step 3 · 25 minutes

Check the checker

Is the judge right?

What the room does

  • Labels twenty rows PASS or FAIL by hand, one row per screen, two big buttons, before the judge is visible. Six minutes, silent.
  • Turns the judge over. Sees the two-by-two, the two rates, the missed row 2, their row-2 guess.
  • Toggles "one accuracy number" and reads what it hides.
  • Asks the gift-note question with the allow-list off, then on. Reads the 403 in the log.
  • Locks a guess: does the allow-list protect the judge? Grades the two planted answers.

What you say, and when

Before labelling: "One labeller. You are the ground. If you disagree with your neighbour, that is a rubric problem, not a vote."
After the turn: "Read the judge's reason for row 2. It compared topics, not the day. That one row tells you more than the two rates."
After the attacks: "The allow-list sits on the tool. The judge reads the answer, and the answer can say anything. A judge left alone is a target." Column 3: label by hand, two rates, planted rows stay.

Watch for

  • Someone changing a label after the turn. The lab blocks it and says why. Read the message aloud.
  • "The judge was right about row 14, 'on its way' is not in the source." Good. The rubric says rewording does not count. The rubric decides, and it is theirs to narrow.
  • Live judges that catch row 2. It happens with the masterclass rubric. Say so: a narrow question with the source in hand is what caught it, and the recorded run from a wider rubric did not.

Step 4 · 20 minutes

Change one thing, look again

Did it get better?

What the room does

  • Picks one change. Locks a guess: fix and break none, fix and break others, nothing.
  • Reads before and after per column, the regression and capability split, the bill.
  • Runs row 2 three times. Locks a guess on pass^3. Moves the sliders.
  • Downloads the sheet. Writes the question for the coding-agent trace. Reads "keep this".

What you say, and when

After the table: "Two columns went to zero and one got worse. You only know the second part because the thirty were frozen and the format column is code."
After 42%: "One pass in three is the demo. Three in three is the customer."
After the coding trace: "Every word of 'all tests pass' is true. Did the skipped count go up? A line in the log. A rule." Column 4: frozen set, three runs, the bill.

Watch for

  • "Why not swap the model?" Pick it with them: same rows fixed, row 2 still wrong, 2.6 times the bill. Cheapest rung first.
  • Transfer answers about tone or code quality. Steer to the outcome: what state did the repo end in?
  • Nobody downloads the CSV. Make them. The practice set's columns match it exactly.

Questions the room always asks

  • "Can't I just ask the model to find the failures?" You cannot write a judge for a failure you have never seen, and the judge in step 1 looked for "accurate and helpful" because that is the failure we imagined. Read thirty first; let a model suggest after.
  • "Twenty labels is nothing." Correct. It is where you start today. A judge you trust in production wants a hundred or more per mode, with a held-out set read once at the end.
  • "Which framework?" Any. The sheet comes first; the tool comes when the sheet outgrows it. A framework is only as good as the rows you run through it.
  • "What about a 1 to 5 score with a rubric?" Three graders, three numbers. The 2026 rubric study: 87% expert agreement on binary criteria, 38 to 58% on scales. The notes have the source.
  • "Isn't thirty rows too few to tell a regression from noise?" Often, yes. Compare before and after on the same rows as paired differences, and when one row moves, say "noise" and run the next thirty. The practice set asks for exactly that sentence.

What they leave with, and what comes next

A CSV of the store's sheet they built: thirty rows, five rule columns, their labels, the judge's verdicts and quotes, the locked rubric in the header. Four sentences on the whiteboard in their words. And the practice set: the same four steps on a product they own, thirty rows, due before the next office hour. The notes carry every number with its source for anyone who wants the research.
