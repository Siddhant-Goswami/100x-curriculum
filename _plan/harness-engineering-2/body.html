Harness Engineering 101 ended with a loop: you write a goal, and an LLM writes the prompts. This session asked what comes after that. Most of it was work you do by hand, before any code runs.

The code is in the repo. Section 11 tells you how to start.

01Three levels, in five minutes

We started with a recap, for anyone who missed the first session.

  • Level 1, the prompt. In 2023 and 2024 the skill was writing better prompts.
  • Level 2, the context. What an LLM could read grew from about 8,000 tokens to over 1 million. So we connected tools to it (MCP) and wrote a one-page file on how to use each tool (a skill).
  • Level 3, the harness. From late 2025, an LLM could hold one task for hours. The system around it now decides what it may touch and where it runs.

The picture we used was a horse. It has its own power. To get from A to B over a long distance, you need a harness.

Claude Code is one harness. We opened it in an earlier session and found nothing fancy inside. It can read, write and edit files, search them, run commands, search the web, start other agents, ask you a question, and connect to tools. Around those sit the rules and the permissions.

But at every level you were still the one checking the output.

02What stops it rewriting the harness?

The fix from the first session was a loop. You stop writing prompts. You write three things once, and an LLM writes the prompts against them.

A verifier is the way you check an output against what you call success. Then came the question for the session.

"The loop already rewrites its prompts against your verifier. What stops it from rewriting the harness?"

The chat answered: permissions, access, guardrails, and "if the harness breaks, the system breaks".

All of those are real. A loop that is scored by one check will find the cheapest way to pass that check. So the answer is two decisions, and both are yours.

  • What may it touch? It may change the harness. It may never open the verifier.
  • Who decides a change stays? Plain code and your labels decide. The LLM that made the change does not.

This is a problem of decisions before it is a problem of permissions.

So the first thing to build is the verifier. And a good verifier starts with a question about time.

03When can you grade a brief?

The example for the session was an agent our team uses. It reads the links you saved this week and writes a brief in three parts: what changed, who disagrees, and what to do.

We asked the room: you receive this brief today. When is the right time to grade it? Today, in 3 days, in 7, in 30?

Grade it today and you will fail. On the day news reaches you, everything feels important. You cannot yet know whether it helped.

Now open your saved folder and look at what you saved 30 days ago. You can tell at once which posts you used and which you never opened again.

The first report is not the truth. It is one likely signal.

So the verifier is built from old links. 7 days works. 30 days is the threshold we use.

04Label five of your own links

We did this live. A link you acted on is signal. A link you never used is noise. Open the saved folder of the platform you use most (X, Instagram, YouTube, Reddit, or your browser history). Take links from last month and give each one a label.

LabelMeaningFrom the session
SignalYou acted on it and it helped.Cloudflare's post on code mode. It went into one of our tools a week later and cut its cost.
NoiseYou never used it."Harness is dead" threads. Acting on them would have cancelled this session.
Not sure yetYou did not act and cannot tell.Leave it unlabelled for now.

In our own saves, fewer than 15 of every 100 links were signal. One person in the chat counted 10.

The goal of the agent is to turn that ratio around: most of what reaches you is something you will use.

Do not hand this step to an LLM. An LLM can say what is useful in general. It cannot say what was useful to you, because it has not lived your month.

From the chat: "How do I check whether I acted on it?"

Ask what you did afterwards. "I read it, and used it in the next lecture" is acted. "I saved it and it felt important" is not.

From the chat: "I do not save anything."

Use your browser history. It lists the links you opened, which works just as well.

05Put the labels in one file

You do not have to collect the links by hand. Ask a tool that can use your browser to do it. This is the prompt we showed, tidied:

copy and paste
Open my bookmarks on X. For every post I saved in the last 60 days,
write one row: date saved, who posted it, the topic in one line, the link.
Give me a CSV with the columns: date, source, title, url, label.
Leave the label column empty. Do not judge the posts.

Notice what the prompt leaves out. It does not ask for labels. Then you go through the file row by row and fill the last column yourself. It is tiring, and it is where your judgement goes in.

That file of labelled examples, plus a rule for scoring against it, is the verifier. Some people call it an eval. We kept to "verifier".

There is an optional second file for decisions: something you had to decide that month, and how it turned out. Skip it on your first try.

06Score with three numbers

How do you turn a brief into a number? We started with three rules.

The brief did thisPoints
Included a link that mattered+1
Called a decision right+2
Called a noise link important-0.5

The week on the slide: the brief found 7 of the 9 links that mattered. It called both decisions right. It called 3 noise links important.

So it earned 7 + 4 - 1.5 = 9.5 points, out of a possible 9 + 4 = 13. That is 0.73.

Why the penalty? Without it, the cheapest way to find every link that matters is to include every link.

These three numbers are a starting template. Change them once you have seen your own results.

07Fifteen lines for the first agent

Do not build a harness. Take one that exists: Claude Code, Codex, or Hermes if you want open source. Then spend your time on what it cannot do for you.

We opened the first agent's instruction file on screen. It is about 15 lines.

You write a short weekly brief for one reader,
from the links they saved that week.

Write the brief in three parts:
## What changed
## Who disagrees
## What to do

Cite every link you mention by its id, like [L014].

For each decision, end with one line:
D1: adopt      D2: wait      D3: ignore
  • Adopt. Act on it now.
  • Wait. Let it settle, then decide.
  • Ignore. It is noise.

The three parts of the brief are a human choice. They work for our team. Change them if they do not work for you.

In Claude Code this file is CLAUDE.md. In Codex or Hermes, name it AGENTS.md. (The repo's loop keeps one CLAUDE.md and hands it to whichever harness you run.)

08Correct it by hand, round after round

Now test the agent on the past. Give it the same old links with your labels removed. Ask it to label each one. Then put its answers next to yours.

Where it disagrees with you, write down what it missed and what to do next time. Give that note back and run again. On the whiteboard these were "the learning after run 1", "after run 2", and so on.

The second run makes fewer mistakes, and some new ones. Write those down too.

Finding a mistake is the easy half. Another LLM could do it. Saying how to do better next time is the half that stays human.

How many rounds? For our team it took 23 before we trusted 95 of every 100 labels. For you it may be 3 or 10. Stop when you are satisfied.

You cannot write down a judgement you have not made. So look at the output first.

Those notes, in order, are a record of how you teach. What could read them?

09Hand the correcting to a second agent

You do not need a new tool for this. You start a new session with a second instruction file. We called this agent the optimizer.

Its only job is to improve the first agent's setup. This is the start of its file:

You improve another agent's setup. That agent writes a weekly
brief for one reader. You never write a brief and you never
score one. Plain code does the scoring.

Each round:
1. Read the report.
2. Open the raw trace of the worst week. Find where the miss happened.
3. Read the journal, so you do not repeat a change that was undone.
4. Make ONE change, small enough to review in a minute.
5. End with: REASON (what you changed) and PREDICT (what it should fix).

A trace is the full log of one run: every step the first agent took and what came back. The optimizer reads it the way you read the output in section 08.

So the second agent does the job you were doing by hand. The idea does not depend on the task. Any work where you can state the goal, state success and check it can take the same shape.

From the Q&A: "I am a designer. How does this help me?"

The tools, the place it runs and the rules change. The loop does not. You still define the goal, define success, and show how you correct the output.

From the Q&A: who should a company hire for this?

Hire for the judgement, then teach the harness. Our best verifiers were written by people who design education programmes and do not write code.

10What we did not run: the loop

We ran out of time before running the two agents together. The repo has a finished run, on a made-up sample month of 100 links. Here it is.

Round 1 is the one to look at. Reading every link sounded sensible. The agent then cited everything, the noise penalty hit, and code undid the change. Nobody had to argue with it.

That is the answer to the question in section 02. The optimizer may rewrite the harness because it cannot reach the labels, and because it does not decide what stays.

One more check. The loop only ever saw weeks 1 to 3. Week 4 stayed closed until the end, then was scored once.

The sample is small and made up, including the product names in it. Your own month will be messier, and a single run can score very differently from the next. The repo averages three runs per week for that reason.

Check yourself. Answer each one aloud before you open it.

A friend labels the links they saved yesterday. What is wrong with that?

They cannot yet know which ones helped. Everything saved yesterday still feels important. Use links from about 30 days ago.

You remove the -0.5 penalty. What will the optimizer's first good idea be?

Include every link. That finds all the links that matter and costs nothing. It is what round 1 did, and the penalty is why it was undone.

The score on weeks 1 to 3 rises and week 4 does not move. What happened?

The changes fitted the weeks the loop could see. They did not capture how you decide. Do not trust them on new links.

Words from this session
Harness
The system around an LLM: its tools, where it runs, and its rules.
Verifier
How you check an output against what you call success. Here: your labelled links and a scoring rule.
Brief
What the first agent writes from a week of links: what changed, who disagrees, what to do.
Signal and noise
A link you acted on, and a link you never used.
Decision
Something you had to decide that month, and how it turned out: adopt, wait or ignore.
Trace
The full log of one run: every step the agent took and what came back.
Optimizer
A second agent whose only job is to improve the first agent's setup.

Keep this

An LLM can rewrite its own harness once two things are out of its reach: the labels, and the decision to keep a change. You supply the labels, from links old enough to judge, and you correct it by hand first. A second agent can then take over the correcting.
