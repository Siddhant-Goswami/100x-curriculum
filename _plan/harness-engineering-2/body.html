The first session ended with a loop: you write the goal, and an LLM writes the prompts. This session asked what comes next. Most of the answer is work you do by hand.

All the code is in the repo.

01Three levels, in five minutes

We started with a recap.

  • Prompt. You got better results by writing better words.
  • Context. LLMs could read far more text, so we gave them tools, plus a short note on how to use each one.
  • Harness. LLMs could stay on one task for hours. Now the system around the LLM matters most: what it may touch, and where it runs.

Think of a horse. It is strong on its own, but to pull a cart a long way it needs a harness. Claude Code is one harness for an LLM: it reads and edits files, runs commands and searches the web, inside rules and permissions you set.

But at every level, you were still the one checking the output.

02What stops it rewriting the harness?

The first session's fix was a loop. You write three things once, and an LLM writes the prompts against them.

The third thing is your way of checking an output. We call it the verifier. Then came the question for the session.

"The loop already rewrites its prompts against your verifier. What stops it from rewriting the harness?"

The chat said: permissions, guardrails. True, but there is a catch. A loop scored by one check will find the cheapest way to pass that check. So two questions are yours to answer.

  • What may it touch? The harness, yes. The verifier, never.
  • Who decides a change stays? Plain code and your labels. Not the LLM that made the change.

It is a problem of decisions before it is a problem of permissions.

So build the verifier first. It starts with a question about time.

03When can you grade a brief?

Our example: an agent that reads the links you saved this week and writes a short report: what changed, who disagrees, and what to do. We call it a brief.

You get the brief today. When should you grade it: today, in 3 days, in 7, or in 30?

Not today. On the day you save something, it all feels important. Now look at what you saved 30 days ago. You can tell at once which ones you used.

The first report is not the truth. It is one likely signal.

So the verifier is built from old links. 7 days works; we use 30.

04Label five of your own links

We did this live. Open the saved folder you use most (X, YouTube, Reddit, or your browser history). Take links from last month and label each one.

LabelMeaningExample
SignalYou acted on it and it helped.A post on cutting tool costs. We used it a week later.
Should haveYou did not act, and now wish you had. Optional.A thread with real measurements you skipped.
NoiseYou never used it."Harness is dead" threads.
Not sureYou cannot tell yet.Leave it blank. It is never scored.

In our own saves, fewer than 15 in every 100 links were signal. The agent's job is to flip that: most of what reaches you should be something you use.

Do not hand this step to an LLM. It knows what is useful in general, not what was useful to you.

From the chat: "How do I know if I acted on it?"

Ask what you did next. "I used it in my next lecture" is acted. "I saved it and it felt important" is not.

From the chat: "I don't save anything."

Use your browser history. It works just as well.

05Put the labels in one file

Let a tool that can use your browser collect the links. This is the prompt:

copy and paste
Open my bookmarks on X. For every post I saved 30 to 60 days ago, write one row:
the date, who posted it, the post's first line, the link, and the full text of the post
(with any post it quotes). Give me a CSV with the columns: date, source, title, url, text.
Do not judge the posts.

Why the text? A post on X sits behind a login, so an agent that opens the link sees nothing. With the text in the file, it reads the post itself.

It collects; it does not label. Two commands take it from there. python3 loop.py import reads the file, whatever its columns are called. python3 loop.py label shows you each link and asks for one key: signal, should have or noise. That is where your judgement goes in.

Your labelled file plus a rule for scoring against it: that is the verifier.

Optional: a second file of decisions you faced that month, and how each turned out. Skip it on your first try.

06Score with three numbers

How do you turn a brief into a number? Three rules.

The briefPoints
Included a link that mattered+1
Called a decision right+2
Called a noise link important-0.5

One week: the brief found 7 of the 9 links that mattered, got both decisions right, and pushed 3 noise links. That is 7 + 4 - 1.5 = 9.5 out of 13, or 0.73.

Why the penalty? Without it, the easiest way to find every good link is to include every link.

Treat these numbers as a start. Change them once you see your own results.

07One short file for the first agent

Do not build a harness. Use one that exists (Claude Code, Codex, or Hermes if you want open source) and spend your time on its instructions. The core of ours:

You write a short weekly brief for one reader,
from the links they saved that week.

Write the brief in three parts, with these exact headings:
## What changed
## Who disagrees
## What to do

Cite a link by its id in square brackets, like [L014], when it matters.
Leave out the links that don't, even in a "skip these" line.

If the prompt asks about decisions, end with one line per decision:
D1: adopt      D2: wait      D3: ignore

You start in this folder. Never write `..`, `cd` or an absolute path.
Run one command per shell call.

Adopt means act now. Wait means decide later. Ignore means it is noise.

The last lines matter. Your labels sit one folder up, so a run that looks above its own folder scores zero. Without them, an agent on a real month explored first (ls ..), was refused, and often wrote nothing.

The three parts suit our team. Change them if they do not suit you. The file is harness/CLAUDE.md, and the loop hands the same file to Codex and Hermes. Using Codex by hand? Copy it to AGENTS.md.

08Correct it by hand, round after round

Now test the agent on the past. Give it the same old links with your labels removed, ask it to label them, and compare.

Where it disagrees, write what it missed and what to do next time. Give it that note and run again. Each run makes fewer mistakes, and some new ones. Write those down too.

Here is a real month, which ships with the repo: 109 X bookmarks, 76 of them in weeks 1 to 3, 13 of those signal. Each round adds one rule, then a model labels the same links blind again:

RoundThe rule addedSignal foundNoise called signal
1None yet13 of 1315 of 63
2F1: a demo of what a model can make (a video, a design) is noise10 of 134
3F2: a hype repost of someone else's work is noise10 of 131
4F3: a tool for your own agent setup is signal, whatever it is shown on13 of 134

F1 fixed its 15 and threw out three links the reader had used. F3 brought them back and let a few demos in again. A rule moves the line everywhere, not only where you aimed it.

Spotting a mistake is the easy half; another LLM could do it. Saying how to do better is the half that stays yours.

How many rounds? We needed 23 before we trusted 95 in every 100 labels. You may need 3 or 10. Stop when you are satisfied.

You cannot write down a judgement you have not made. Look at the output first.

Those notes, in order, record how you teach. Could something else read them?

09Hand the correcting to a second agent

Yes: a second agent, with its own instruction file. No new tool needed. We call it the optimizer. Its only job is to improve the first agent's setup.

You improve another agent's setup. That agent writes a weekly
brief for one reader. You never write a brief and you never
score one. Plain code does the scoring.

Each round:
1. Read the report.
2. Open the raw trace of the worst week. Find where the miss happened.
3. Read the journal, so you do not repeat a change that was reverted.
4. Make ONE change, small enough that a person can review it in a minute.
5. End with two lines:
   REASON: what you changed and why
   PREDICT: which misses this should fix

A trace is the log of one run: every step the first agent took, and what came back. The optimizer reads it the way you read the output in section 08.

So the second agent does the job you did by hand. This works for any task where you can state the goal, say what success is, and check it.

From the Q&A: "I am a designer. How does this help me?"

The tools and rules change. The loop does not: you still set the goal, define success, and show how you correct the output.

From the Q&A: who should a company hire for this?

Hire for judgement, then teach the harness. Our best verifiers were written by people who design courses and do not code.

10The loop: both agents together

Now run both agents together. Here is a full run on the real month from section 08, in Claude Code, starting from the three rules written by hand.

Look at rounds 3 and 4. Both tried to rescue short posts that attach an article, and both scored lower, so code undid them. Round 5 found the better line: judge a post by what its author says. Nobody had to argue.

That answers section 02. The optimizer may rewrite the harness because it cannot see your labels and does not decide what stays.

One more check. The loop only saw weeks 1 to 3. Week 4 stayed hidden until the end, then was scored once.

Weeks 1 to 30.7520.986

Week 4 rose, but less than weeks 1 to 3. Read how: the new setup found 8.3 of 9 links that mattered instead of 8.0, and cited a little less noise. Part of what the loop learned carried over to a week it never saw; part only fit the weeks it could see. final prints both numbers under the scores, so you can tell which.

A month is a small sample, and two runs can score quite differently, so the repo averages three runs per week.

The whole run is on the repo's real-month branch: every round as a commit, the rules it kept and every trace. The README says how to read it without switching to it.

Check yourself. Answer each one aloud before you open it.

A friend labels the links they saved yesterday. What is wrong?

They cannot know yet which ones helped. Use links from about 30 days ago.

You remove the -0.5 penalty. What will the optimizer try first?

Include every link. It finds all the good ones and costs nothing. The penalty is what makes that lose.

Weeks 1 to 3 rise and week 4 does not move. What happened?

The changes fit the weeks the loop could see, not how you decide. Do not trust them on new links.

Week 4 rises, but the links it found stay the same and only the noise falls. What did the loop learn?

What to leave out of a brief. Useful, but it is about writing, not about what you value.

Verifier
Your way of checking an output. Here: your labelled links and a scoring rule.
Brief

Signal
A link you acted on and that helped.
Noise
A link you never used.

Something you had to decide that month: adopt, wait or ignore.
