    What you now know
    The checker is a thing that can be wrong, and a thing that can be told what to say. Label by hand before you trust it, report the two rates and the row it missed, and keep the planted rows in the set. Now you have thirty rows and a check for each. Change one thing.
    
      
    Why the stop must be in code, and when the row is wrong
    No prompt reliably detects an injection; the model cannot tell data from commands, so an allow-list and real permissions on the tool are the only defence that holds. The same planted text reaches the judge. And the other direction: a model found a policy loophole on a flight-booking benchmark, the grader marked it a fail, and the outcome was better for the user. When a model "fails" a row and you agree with the model, the row is wrong, not the model.

    Why freeze, why pass^k, and why the bill
    Do not add the row that embarrassed you and quietly drop the one that always passes, or the suite learns your changes and stops measuring them. New rows go in a separate tab and get promoted at the next freeze. Anthropic splits suites in two: a regression suite near 100% and a capability suite that starts low. pass@k is the chance of at least one success in k tries; pass^k is the chance all k succeed. At 75% per run, pass^3 is 42%. Reset state between trials. Fix at the cheapest layer that moves the number, and write the cost next to the result.
    
    
    Further, one line each. Frameworks (Harbor, Braintrust, LangSmith, Langfuse, Arize) are only as good as the rows you run through them. No source of truth to hand the judge: compare before and after in blind pairs, the way GDPval graded expert work. Automated evals are one layer: production monitoring for ground truth, a few transcripts read every week, a human study when you calibrate the judge. Source: Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents.
