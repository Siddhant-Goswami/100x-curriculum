Your RAG app, part by part.

The room's doubts about the RAG app: what each part does, why build it at all, and why an off-topic question still came back with sources.

Missed the office hour? This is what the room asked, and the answers. In Lecture 13 you derived RAG. In the 21 November practical you built a RAG app: it answers questions about a Porsche manual and cites the pages.

01How did it cut my manual?

Someone's app worked and cited the right pages. But in Supabase they could not see how the manual had been split. How did the app cut it into chunks?

The prompt from the practical asks for cuts by meaning: a chunk ends where a sentence and its context end. To see what your own build did, ask the coding tool that wrote it (Antigravity, for most of the room).

Is there one right chunk size? No. Some manuals need two sections read together. With 10 or 15 documents, page 1 of one may connect to a page deep in another. So load a document you know well, and try sizes until the answers hold up.

There is no rule of thumb for chunks. Test on a document you know.

02Is RAG right for my research papers?

Someone wants each hard research paper turned into a simpler version: a newsletter, a blog, plain notes. Is RAG the right way?

The instructor asked back: can ChatGPT or Gemini not do that the moment you upload the file? RAG simplifies nothing. It cuts the text into chunks, indexes them, and hands the right ones to the LLM when a question comes.

A paper is 50 or 100 pages at most. That fits inside the context window, so there is nothing to search for. Finding patterns across a huge pile of papers is a different job.

Ask first why RAG exists. Then ask whether your case has that problem.

Lecture 13 gave the reason: the data stops fitting, or costs too much to paste.

03A method, or a system?

The same learner had a longer aim. Keep every paper, so that later the app can say: this paper matters for the project you are on now.

The instructor said that fits the coming lecture better. It is about a second brain, where you draw on your own past know-how.

The learner guessed how the two relate: RAG is a method, and a second brain is a system. The instructor did not rule on it. He said a second brain is "more to do with your memory", and that you can decide better after the session. Hold the guess until Lecture 15.

04What each part does

From the room: "Why does Groq exist?" And in the practical's chat, some people thought gte-small came from Groq. So here is each part by its job.

  • Groq serves the LLM. Groq is a company that runs LLMs on its own chips, built for speed. It hosts a few open LLMs, ones anyone may download (GPT-OSS is one). Its free key needs no credit card. It is not Grok, the chatbot on X.
  • gte-small turns text into numbers. It is a small program of its own, listed on Hugging Face, and has nothing to do with Groq. It turns each chunk into a list of numbers that places its meaning.
  • Supabase stores them. Supabase hosts a Postgres database. pgvector is an extension you switch on inside Postgres, not a second database. With it, Postgres can store each list of numbers beside the chunk's text and page number, and find the lists closest to a question's.

When you ask a question, the same gte-small turns it into numbers. pgvector finds the closest chunks. The server sends them, with the question, to the LLM on Groq, which answers and cites the pages.

The build guide for the car manual app names another small embedding model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2. Whichever one your app uses, the job is the same, and it does not come from Groq.

Does pgvector do the chunking? No. It cuts nothing and converts nothing. It stores and it searches.

Can I run the LLM without Groq?

Yes. Ollama is a program that runs open LLMs on your own machine. Several open LLMs carry the Apache 2.0 licence, which allows commercial use.

05Why build it at all?

Someone had uploaded their car's manuals to Claude and asked questions there. It felt more interactive. So why build the app?

The instructor agreed with the facts. RAG came about when context windows were small. Today's LLMs can read a document of about 1,400 to 1,500 pages and cite from it. Not quite as well as RAG, he said, but well enough. The companies that make LLMs are racing to make RAG unnecessary.

"So this matters when my data is much bigger?" Yes, and for one more reason. An app needs its own way to store its files and find the right part of them. If that lives inside one company's LLM, your app depends on that LLM. If it is yours, you can swap the LLM and keep everything else.

The LLM can be swapped. The storing and the finding stay yours.

06Best biryani, with sources

One learner asked the Porsche app: "Where is the best biryani?" It answered, and listed sources from the manual, one about battery coding. Typing only "biryani" gave the same sources. Why?

  • The search always hands something back. It returns a set number of chunks, called top-k (the instructor guessed 6 to 8 for this build). It returns the k closest even when none is close, so here they are still car chunks.
  • Nothing told the LLM to stay on the manual. The manual is added context, and the LLM underneath still knows about food. The fix is a rule in the instruction, called a guardrail: answer only questions about the manual.

Another learner's app found the right answer to a test question but buried it in too much text. Three fixes:

  • Lower top-k. The answer gets shorter, but you can lose chunks that belong together (tyre pressure monitoring and fuel efficiency, in the instructor's example).
  • Add one more step. Ask the LLM to pick the exact answer out of what the search returned.
  • Sharpen the question first. Add precision and context to what the user typed before the search runs.

Keep this

Groq serves the LLM, gte-small turns text into numbers, and Supabase stores and searches them with pgvector. A chat app can read one file for you. An app you build keeps its own storing and finding, so you can swap the LLM. The search always returns its closest chunks, so an off-topic question still gets sources until you add a rule.
