Write the eval

Turn the score you gave by feel into a check anyone could run the same way.

On your own, about 45 minutes. Do it by Thu 12 Nov.

The brief

Take the row you tested in Practice set 1, section 04. Write its eval: the decision it serves, the inputs you will test it on, the hard lines, what a 1 and a 5 look like, and who grades. Then re-score your tested row with it. Everything goes in a new tab of the same sheet.

02Why it gets its own practice set

  • Your score was by feel. Two people can give the same reply a 1 and a 5, and both be sure. A score nobody else can reproduce tells you nothing about whether the next fix worked.
  • It is what decides your Judgment rows. In Lecture 9, a task can go to a model only as far as its output can be checked. For a Judgment task, the eval is the check. Until you write it down, the task stays with you.
  • It is the paid part. The office hour's expert listings (for example on Mercor) ask for one thing above all: the reference answer and the criteria a model's attempt is graded against.
  • You will use it every week from here. The bridge in Lecture 11, retrieval in Lectures 12 and 13, fine-tuning in Lecture 16, and evaluating LLMs in Lecture 18 all end with the same question: did it get better? This is how you will know.

03Do it 0 of 3 ready

Stretch: hand your rubric and three outputs to someone outside your field. If they cannot score without asking you, sharpen your 1 and 5.

04Rules

  • Write it yourself. An LLM writing your rubric gives you the average judge, not yours.
  • One task, done well. One eval you would trust beats five you would not.
  • Hard lines are rules. Anything a formula or a few lines of code can check goes there, not in the quality layer.
  • Company data stays on your machine. Use a stand-in with the same shape if you need to.
