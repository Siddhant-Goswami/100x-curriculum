When software stops waiting for a click

Questions on Stitch and on picking the right LLM, then one idea: what an interface is for once you can say what you want.

Missed the office hour? This is what mattered from it. It stands on Lecture 2, where an interface was the bridge between two systems, and Lecture 3, where you built one.

01Three questions about Stitch

The room had tried Stitch, Google's design tool, since the last session. Three questions came back.

  • "Can I make an iPhone app?" Yes. AI Studio writes the app so that it opens in Xcode, Apple's app-building tool, on a Mac. Xcode makes the build file, and the App Store needs a paid Apple developer account. The next session covers it.
  • "I am not happy with the designs Stitch gives me." Sketch the screen on paper and photograph it. Upload the photo with style references from Dribbble or Pinterest.
  • "Why Stitch and not another tool?" Because it is one path: Stitch for the design, AI Studio for the app, Antigravity for the finished product.

02A nail does not need a bulldozer

Someone asked whether the biggest LLM could edit video. It can, the instructor said, but that is "like hitting a nail with a bulldozer". A few seconds of footage could eat a month of credits.

Then a harder case from the room. Three years of customer emails, most with a PDF or an Excel file attached. The goal: AI reads them all and learns how customers ask for things. "A cloth to keep the mouse on" means a mousepad. The coding tool used up its limit in hours, before it had read one week of mail.

"I don't know which model I have to use."

The instructor's answer: the trouble is how the job is split, not which LLM does it. A big LLM charges for every click: open the list, open the email, open the file. So split the job.

Part of the jobWhat does it
Open each email, download the filePlain automation: a script that repeats the same clicks (Selenium is one). No LLM, so no credits.
Get the text out of a clear PDFSoftware that turns a picture of text into text (optical character recognition, OCR).
Read a PDF that is not clear textAn LLM that reads images.
Sort each request into a categoryA small, fast LLM that only picks from a short list. It reads; it does not take actions.

How do you judge which LLM a task needs? No fixed rule: you experiment. Ask what kind of thinking the task needs (text, maths, pictures). About 90% of the instructor's own live products still run fine on GPT 3.5, an old, cheap LLM.

Split the job first. Then give each part the smallest tool that does it.

From the room: which problems in AI are still open to build on?

The labs spend billions to build the intelligence, but the public cannot do much with it raw. Someone has to turn it into things people can use. That is this room: the most value is in applying the intelligence, not in building it.

03One table, booked two ways

The session's topic opened with two screens side by side. Both book a restaurant table.

The instructor asked the room:

"What if the computer understood what we wanted? Would we still need an interface?"

The chat split between yes and no. The verdict was yes, but for a different job. Say you want to set a budget with a price slider, or see the options ranked, or look at the pictures before anything is booked. Each of those needs a screen. The interface appears where you want to steer.

04Three products for one goal

The same goal, a table tonight, can be built as three different products. What changes is who does the steps.

In the first you choose everything and click: operate. In the second the software does the work and you give the final yes: consult. In the third you say it and it is done: delegate.

Which one is the future? The instructor's answer: a mix of all three, moving towards the third over time.

05What may it do without asking?

Design used to start from one question: what should happen when the user clicks? The instructor swapped it: what should happen when the user does not have to click?

So you draw the limits before you build:

  1. What can it do without asking?
  2. What needs your confirmation?
  3. What must never happen unless you say so?
  4. What does it do when it is unsure?

The slide then ran the dinner through six checks.

The checkFor the dinner
What do you want?Dinner for 4. Any open table will not do.
What is it allowed to do?It may reserve. It may not pay: a human pays.
Can you follow what it did?It allowed for traffic and moved the booking to a later slot.
Is the step the right size?It drafts the email first. It does not send it straight away.
Can you undo it?Any change it made can be reversed.
Is there a record?It logs what it did and takes your feedback, so the same mistake is not repeated.

The instructor called the fifth check, undo, "a superpower".

06Why good apps still take time

The last question from the room: if AI can build anything, why do the apps we use still take so long to ship updates? Wispr Flow's note-taker, for one, came to Mac first and Windows later.

The instructor's answer: putting up a building has become quick. The architect's job, deciding what to build, still takes time. Working out what the user wants is the slow part, and AI is poor at it.

"AI can do a million things, but that million things is noise."

What changed for him since these tools arrived? He barely writes code now. He works out the logic and how the parts fit. So the exact wording of code matters less, and the principles matter more: is it efficient, is it reliable, does it do what you meant?

Words from this lecture
Plain automation
A script that repeats the same steps the same way each time. No LLM in it.
Operate
You choose everything and press the buttons yourself.
Consult
You say what you need, the software does the work, and you give the final yes.
Delegate
You give the instruction and the software does the whole task.

Keep this

When software can act on what you say, the interface does not vanish. It moves to the places where the user wants to steer, confirm or undo. Split every job, and give each part the smallest tool that does it.

07Your turn: your product, no screen

Take the product you are building. How would it behave with no screen, if it already knew what your user wanted? Answer the four questions in section 05 for it. Then mark where your user would still want to steer. Those are the screens you need.
