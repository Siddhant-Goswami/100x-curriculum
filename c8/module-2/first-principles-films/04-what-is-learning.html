Film 4 of 8 · Language Models from First Principles, Part One · 11:05

What is learning?

Give a model "France" and out comes "Paris". Inside are billions of dials. Nobody set one by hand. So who did?

How to play: click the raised pieces in each drawing, or the buttons under it. Reach the goal and the explanation opens.

01A guessing game

In 1951 Claude Shannon showed people a line of text, covered the rest, and asked for the next letter.

What you just found

A language model is a machine playing that game. It does not guess letters. It guesses tokens. And it has to start from what the chip already has: bytes. The word the is stored as 116 104 101. Text on a chip is a row of bytes, nothing else.

02Who decided what the tokens are?

Start with 256 tokens, one per byte. It works, but " the capital of France is" takes 25 guesses. For bigger tokens, do not ask a person. Count.

What you just found

That is byte pair encoding. About fifty thousand merges later, those 25 bytes are 5 tokens. Common words are one token, rare words stay in parts, and a word nobody has written can still be spelled byte by byte. Nothing is ever unknown.

A byte is a pattern we agreed on. A token is a pattern a machine counted.

03The first language model was a count

Read a pile of text. For every token, note what came right after it, and turn the counts into chances. To guess, open the row and pick. For decades, this was a language model. Ask it to finish the capital of France is.

What you just found

It is an exact memory again: the table of past house sales. Ask for a house nobody sold and it says not found. The line could answer. The table never can.

Principle SixA count only knows what it saw. A pattern can guess what it didn't.

So swap the table for dials, as with the houses:

  • Tokens go in. Out comes a chance for every token, even after words it has never seen together. That wall of dials is a model.
  • Each dial scales the number coming in. Between dials sits a gate: below zero, nothing passes. Without gates, a billion scalings would still be a straight line. The gates let it bend.
  • Then pick one: usually the likeliest, sometimes a roll of the dice. That is why the same question can get two answers.

04Which way to turn each dial

A new wall of dials starts random. Cover the last token of a real sentence, let it guess, uncover. It gave Paris a 2% chance. The score for how far off is the loss. Lower is better. Picture the loss as a landscape, with you standing on it in fog.

What you just found

The slope under your feet is the gradient. A bit of maths works backwards from the mistake and hands every dial its direction at once. Walking down it is gradient descent.

In a real model every step is small, because the same dials serve every sentence. Crank one to make "Paris" certain and a thousand other sentences get worse.

05Training

Attempt, measure the mistake, feed it back. Then the next sentence. A trillion times. Along the way, what the model writes changes.

What you just found

"France" and "Paris" sat side by side in millions of sentences. Each time, the loss got a little lower. That is how the dials came to know. Rule of thumb: about twenty tokens of reading for every dial. Recent models read more than 15 trillion.

06What learning needs

Plot the house line's mistakes, a child's falls while learning to walk, and the model's loss. All three slope down. That slope is learning, and it needs four things. Call this the card. It comes back in every film from here.

What you just found

Principle SevenLearning is reducing mistakes toward a goal, with feedback from every attempt.

So who set the dials? The text did. Cover a token, guess, uncover: the text grades itself. That is self-supervised learning. And since the dials blend everything they read, the only place to choose what goes in is before training.

07In your build

  • Tokens are what you are billed for and what the context limit counts.
  • The temperature setting in the Groq playground is the dice.
  • You cannot add a fact to the dials. You put it in the prompt, as in Lecture 10.

08Check yourself

Answer in your head, then open it.

Why start from bytes and not a dictionary of words?

Any text on a chip is already bytes, so every word, even a new one, can be spelled. Nothing is unknown.

What is the count table's one rule, and why does it lose "Paris"?

Open the row for the last token. The last token is "is", and that row mixes every sentence that ever had "is" in it.

Why not make the table look at the last ten tokens?

It would need more rows than grains of sand, and almost all would be empty: not found.

What do the gates between dials add?

They let the chain bend. Scaling alone, however many times, is still a straight line.

Why is each nudge small?

The same dials serve every sentence, so a big turn for one breaks many others.

Who wrote the answer key in this stage of training?

Nobody. The text did: the covered token is the answer.
