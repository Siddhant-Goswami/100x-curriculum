Post-read notes · Lecture 9

Verifiable means automatable

From a hallucinated track list to the question every job now has to answer. The model stayed closed tonight; the lecture was about where the line between rules and prediction sits.

01Six weeks on one whiteboard

  • Client is the first interface: the browser, the device, the UI a person touches.
  • Server holds the business logic. For us, the diagnoser that turns a workflow into an automation plan for Aarav.
  • API is the second interface: address, action, package, key. Data lives in files, then in a database.
  • All of that is orange: deterministic, same input, same output.
  • One box is purple: the LLM, GPT OSS on GroqCloud, called through an API like any other service. ChatGPT has the same ratio: a POST with the prompt, a status code back, and Postgres underneath at over a billion users.
  • Drawing this for a new problem is the HLD round. A student interviewing at Anthropic was asked to design a web research agent: inputs, outputs, client, server, data, and where the LLM is actually needed. No code.

The model is the useful part. The orange system around it is what makes it usable.

02Why the second mountain is steeper

  • From about 5 out of 10 to about 8. An estimate from seven cohorts and roughly 2,500 students.
  • A UI breaks where you can see it. A model gives a different answer tomorrow, after an update, or for another user. Even its builders cannot fully say why; that is its own field, interpretability.
  • Answers that shift are normal, not a sign you broke something. The series stays practical; research depth goes in extra resources.
  • The most common mistake in past cohorts: reaching for the LLM first, even for jobs it is bad at, like storing information.

Start left of the line. Move a step into the purple box only when you cannot verify its output with rules. Given two equal solutions, the rule-based one wins: reproducible, cheaper, predictable at scale.

03Input and output of the bare model

  • Same framing as the UI and the API: input, process, output.
  • The chat said tokens, vectors, embeddings, prompts. Each one got the same request: define it in one line a peer can follow. A term you cannot unpack hides the mechanism. Embeddings are not what you hand the model.
  • The answer we could check live: open GPT OSS 120B in the Groq playground, type text, get text back. Text in, text out. Some models take images and audio; for our reference model, this is the verified truth, and we build from verified truth.

04The query that broke it

Which track should I choose in 100x Engineers cohort?
  • The shape was right. A decision framework, assessment questions, a mapping from answers to tracks, a checklist. Close to what a good mentor would give.
  • The data was wrong. Front-end, back-end, full-stack, data and ML, DevOps, product, security. None of them exist.
  • The real choice has two axes. A career track (entrepreneurship or career acceleration, set by your North Star) and an execution path (code, low-code, no-code, set by your background).
  • We fixed the constraint: one answer, no follow-up questions. Under that, an assessment framework is the right template.
  • Right template, wrong data: hallucination. It is why this model, as is, cannot be the career coach on the 100x website.

05Can the orange box fix it?

Satyaki's proposal, tested step by step against the line:

  • The design was not wrong. The procedure is the point. Write the solution as steps, place each step on one side of the line. If any step only works with an LLM, the system is probabilistic whether you meant it or not.
  • Kandarp, in chat: if you cannot write the verifier, you cannot build the system.
  • Sampreet: if you do not know what the right answer looks like, find a domain expert before you build.

06The deterministic answer already existed

  • Define the template: how the assessment works, the tracks that actually exist, and a decision framework (this North Star and this background, choose this).
  • Fetch the track data from the database that powers the curriculum, not the website. The website updates slowly; the database is current. One API call, one template, the right answer every time.
  • Cohort 1 did exactly this. No team, no credits: each recurring question got its own decision framework as a plain HTML page. Test it with five real users, as Assignment 1 taught. If they can decide from it, it works.
  • Steps are a process; anyone can copy them. Knowing which side of the line a problem falls on before you design is intuition. Learnable, not teachable. Run it on 30 problem statements over 30 days and it becomes subconscious.

07Where rules run out

  • Same student, now on the code track. The question came from a real test of an AI assistant in Cohort 6.
  • No database holds the answer, no template produces it. It depends on who you are, what you can actually do, the role you want, and the market. Some rule will always be missing.
  • A 100x mentor can still estimate it, because they have watched many students make the journey and formed a judgment.
  • Verification by rule: 1 + 2 is always 3. You can use an LLM; you do not need one.
  • Verification by judgment: whether a customer deserves a refund (from Lecture 1). Grey areas, settled by patterns an experienced support executive learned from past cases. When the check needs judgment, a probabilistic system belongs in the design.

08Can a machine learn judgment?

Humans form judgment from experience. For a machine, the equivalent is data. The room's answers:

  • Past data (Anupama and many others). Necessary. Every FAQ ever answered at 100x is not enough on its own.
  • Data alone breaks down (Varun). Context bloats and models hallucinate more as it grows. Maybe LLMs guide judgment rather than make it.
  • Decision theory (Prateek). Fuzzy multi-criteria decision-making: gather human judgment through surveys, pairwise comparisons and linguistic scales, then turn it into rules. Works for one category of problem, not all.
  • A human in the loop (Satyaki). A person checks the machine's judgment against theirs and corrects it over time.
  • Reward and punishment (Samarth). The right instinct, but you cannot deny a machine a salary. What a reward looks like to a model is open for the coming weeks.

It depends, on two things in a fixed order. Swasti and Mustafa got there first: verification first, data second.

  • Mountains of data, no way to tell right from wrong: the model cannot learn.
  • A perfect verifier, no examples: it cannot learn either. A person who knows the rules still needs worked examples to build intuition.

09The Verifier's Rule, revisited

  • From the first lecture: Jason Wei on the asymmetry of verification. It is now the guiding rule for the probabilistic half.
  • In one line: the ease of training AI on a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is. Every task that is possible to solve and easy to verify will be solved by AI.
  • The benchmarks show it. Graduate STEM exams and competition maths have a correct answer for every question, and decades of past papers. Models ace them.
  • So read benchmarks with care. Acing a verifiable test with enough examples shows the rule working, not general intelligence.

If you can verify a task and the data exists or can be collected, a machine can learn it.

11The 80% finding

  • Across three years of 100x alumni jobs, about 80% of tasks are verifiable. Not automated today: verifiable. Some have the data, some not yet. When the data arrives, and labs are working on exactly that, they become automatable.
  • Six months of asking people hit by layoffs what they actually did: 80% to 90% verifiable, every time.
  • Not a doomsday lecture. It changes what upskilling means. Ten lectures on neural networks change nothing if your tasks are the ones a lab can verify and buy data for. The useful question is which 20% to 30% of your work is not easily verifiable, and how to spend more time there.
  • Karpathy's jagged edge. GPT-4 to GPT-5: the skills that jumped (maths, reading, writing, knowledge) are the verifiable ones.
  • Anthropic's jobs as bundles of tasks, with a nurse. Ordering ward supplies: automated (the list and invoice make it easy to verify). Monitoring patients, discharge instructions, shift plans: augmented. Helping a patient bathe: human. Automated is orange, augmented is purple.
  • If more than 80% of your week sits in the automated band, use this module to shift it.

Before someone else automates your job, verify it yourself.

12Data is bigger than you think

  • A verifier is not automation next quarter. How long a task has left depends on how much data it takes to make a model reliable at it.
  • For scale: FineWeb, a public web crawl, is 44 TB and 15 trillion tokens (words turned into numbers). Roughly the floor for testing even a small language model. GPT-class and Claude-class models use 10 to 100 times more.
  • To experiment tonight, use an open-source model, ideally locally, not a hosted proprietary one (the reason comes in a later session). The instructor's go-to: Qwen 3.5 on Ollama. Needs 6 to 7 GB, runs on a 16 GB laptop, works from Claude Code, OpenCode, Hermes or any terminal tool.

Keep this

An LLM earns its place only where the output cannot be checked by a rule and needs judgment. Whether a machine can learn that judgment depends on two things, in this order: can you verify the output, and do you have the data. Run that test on every task in your own job and you will know which parts are about to be automated, and which stay yours.
