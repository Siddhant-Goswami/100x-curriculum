Find the right few paragraphs.

Five questions about our own notes, and what each one hands you.

The model has never read our notes. So we ask it five questions about our own course, and mark each answer with a rule anyone can check. Each question hands you something different, and that decides how you find the answer.

The questionIt hands you
Q1What is Lecture 6 about?A key: the lecture number
Q2Which lecture teaches Verifier's Law?Exact words
Q3Why is the model making things up?Only a meaning
Q4Which lecture shows how to put our app on the App Store?Nothing: it is not in the notes
Q5Lecture 11 says the model "still named Zapier". In the lecture with the runs, the fix added three real UI builders to the data. Which three?A path: two searches

Every run below is on the Retrieval Bench, with GPT-OSS 120B on Groq. Bring your own Groq key and run them yourself.

01The closed book

Ask all five with nothing attached: 0 out of 5. For Q1 it asked which course we meant. For Q2 it named a lecture at MIT. It sounded just as sure when it was wrong.

A bright student, in a closed-book exam, on a book they never read. There are two ways to open the book: paste the notes in, or give the model a tool, the Lecture 12 move. Q1 carries a lecture number, so a tool can fetch Lecture 6 by its number. The model reads it and answers: databases. Q1 passes.

Fetch the passage, add it to the prompt, let the model write. In that order the three verbs are a name: retrieval, augmented, generation. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG.

You built RAG in Lecture 12. Today: what if the question carries no number?

02Why not paste it all?

All twelve notes pages come to about 30,000 tokens. The model can read about 131,000 at once. It fits. For twelve pages, pasting is a fair design: Anthropic's own guidance says a knowledge base under about 200,000 tokens can go straight into the prompt.

So why not always paste? Pasting has three costs.

  • The bill. Every question sends all 30,000 tokens again, to use two paragraphs. At $0.15 per million input tokens, one question costs $0.0045, and a thousand a day for 30 days costs $135. Send only the two passages that matter, about 1,500 tokens: $6.75.
  • No receipt. The answer does not say which page it came from. You cannot check what you cannot see (Lecture 11).
  • The drift. Answers get less reliable as the prompt grows, long before it is full. Chroma tested 18 models in July 2025, and every one showed it.

On a free Groq key you meet a fourth cost first: Groq refuses the request before the model reads a word. The limit is 8,000 tokens a minute, and the request asks for 31,131.

Rule zero: if it fits, the questions are few, and nobody needs a source, paste it.

It is the first answer in any interview about RAG, and most people skip it. Past rule zero, the job is not to give the model everything. It is to give it the right few paragraphs. How do you find them?

03Find it by its words

Q2: "Which lecture teaches Verifier's Law?" Press Ctrl+F across every passage and type Verifier: 24 passages light up, in 6 lectures. Finding is not enough. You also have to choose which ones to hand over. Type the exact words, Verifier's Law, and 2 passages remain, both in Lecture 1. Q2 passes.

Ctrl+F reads the whole pile every time. That is fine for twelve pages, not for twelve million. A book solves this with the index at the back: a second copy of the book, sorted by word, built once (course lesson 2.6). Searching that copy is keyword search.

Now Q3: "Why is the model making things up?" Search for making things up: 0 passages.

Lecture 10 has a whole section on exactly this question. It never says "making things up". It says hallucination. You would find it anyway, because you know the two mean the same thing. Ctrl+F does not.

Q1 handed us a number. Q2 handed us the exact words. Q3 hands us only a meaning.

04Find it by its meaning

How would a computer match "making things up" to "hallucination"? A list of synonyms, for every phrase in every language, never ends. Asking the model to read everything is pasting it all again. You have met the answer already in course lesson 5.1: a map, where words with close meanings sit close together.

Do the same for passages. A small model reads each passage once and turns it into 384 numbers: its position on a map of meaning. Then place the question on the same map, and take the passages nearest to it.

On the bench, Q3's nearest passage is Lecture 10, "Why models hallucinate", scored 0.87, where 1 would be identical. Hand the nearest passages to the model with Q3 and it points to Lecture 10. Q3 passes.

The 384 numbers are an embedding. One embedding per passage, not per word. How many nearest passages you take is top-k.

What should one dot be: a whole lecture, a paragraph, or a sentence? A whole lecture covers ten ideas, so its dot lands between all of them and near none. A lone sentence says "it does this because of that", and nobody knows what "it" is. The middle is a chunk: the smallest piece that still makes sense alone. On the bench, a chunk is a passage of up to 170 words.

Which steps are rules, and which are a model's guess (Lecture 9)? Placing on the map uses a model, yet the same text always lands in the same place. Only the last step, the model writing the answer, can surprise you.

05Similar is not correct

Q4: "Which lecture shows how to put our app on the App Store?" No notes page does. Search by meaning still returns three passages: Lecture 7's opening (the app's function moves to a rented machine, behind a URL), how the Lecture 4 notes are organised, and Lecture 8's opening (that API on Vercel learns to remember). None of them is about the App Store. The map has no edge, so something is always nearest.

Hand those three to the model and it can answer anyway: "Lecture 7", read off the deploy passage. Putting an app behind a URL is not putting it on the App Store.

Which step failed? The search did what we built it to do. The model did what we asked. The design had no way to say no. Three fixes:

  • The honest miss. Add one line to the prompt: "If the passages do not answer it, say it is not in the notes." With that line it says the notes do not cover it. Q4 passes, and the right answer was a refusal.
  • The receipt. Show the passage beside the answer, so anyone can check it.
  • Check the passage, not the sentence. A fluent answer proves nothing. Verify the record (Lecture 11).

When an answer is wrong, one of two things broke, and the receipt tells you which.

06The path

Q5: 'Lecture 11 says the model "still named Zapier". In the lecture with the runs, the fix added three real UI builders to the data. Which three?'

The first search, still named Zapier, finds two passages: Lecture 11's recap and Lecture 10's table of runs. Neither names the fix. But Lecture 11 says "Lecture 10 has the runs". The second search, UI builders, finds the fix in another Lecture 10 section: Lovable, Google AI Studio and Antigravity.

Could our code write the second search before it reads the first result? No: code does not read. The model writes it. Search is one more tool on the Lecture 12 loop: the model asks, the server searches, the model reads the result and decides whether to search again. On the bench it passed 12 runs out of 12, with two to four searches each.

The cost: every round sends the whole conversation again, passages included. More calls, more tokens, more waiting.

Let the model search in rounds only when the question hands you a path.

07The ladder

The five questions were one question, asked five times: what does the question hand you? Read top to bottom, it is the decision you would make for a client. Three of the seven answers are: you do not need retrieval.

Each rung costs more to build, and is harder to check, than the one above it. A lookup is right or wrong. A keyword hit can be read. A nearest passage is a guess you must test. A model searching in rounds is several guesses in a row. Most real systems use more than one rung: a support bot looks up the order by its number and searches the help articles by meaning, in the same answer.

Six situations, and the rung for each
The situationThe rungWhy
A 20-page HR handbook, 30 questions a dayPasteRule zero

In an interview: "First I ask whether retrieval is needed at all: is it behaviour, does it fit, is it a database question. If it is needed, I ask what the question hands me: a key, exact words, a meaning, or a path. I start at the cheapest rung and only move down when it fails on real questions."

08Read any retrieval diagram

A production diagram is the two-phase picture with more boxes. Ask four questions of every box:

  1. Once, or for every question? Which phase is it in?
  2. Rule or model? Same input, same output, or a guess?
  3. Which rung does it serve? Key, words, meaning, path, or is it a check?
  4. If I delete it, which question on the scoreboard turns red? If none, it is not needed yet.

Question 4 turns reading into judgment. For our twelve pages, most boxes on a production diagram can go.

09Design it, defend it

A design is the two-phase picture plus nine lines, each with a reason. The right column is the course assistant from today:

Line to fillThe course assistant
1Who asks, and one real question they askA learner: "Why is the model making things up?"
2What documents, how many, how often they change12 notes pages, about 30,000 tokens, two new pages a week
3Rule zero: does it fit, and is pasting good enough?It fits today, not once session transcripts are added. Learners need the source.
4What do the questions hand you? Which rungs?Sometimes a lecture number (key). Usually a meaning.
5What is one chunk, and why that size?A passage inside one section. It holds one idea and reads alone.
6What goes in the prompt? What may the model refuse?The question plus 3 passages. It may say "not in the notes".
7What does the user see as the receipt?The lecture number and the passage under the answer
8Three questions with known answers, one absent from the documentsQ2, Q3 and Q4 from the scoreboard
9What breaks first, and how will you notice?A question that shares a word with the wrong lecture. The known-answer score drops.

Lines 3, 8 and 9 are the ones a weak design leaves empty. Fill one card for your own project, or for one of these:

  • The clinic. A 40-page patient handbook. Patients ask questions on WhatsApp, about 50 a day.
  • The shop. 8,000 product pages and an orders table. Customers ask "where is my order?" and "which shoe suits flat feet?"
  • The law firm. 50,000 contracts. Lawyers ask for clause 14.2 of a named contract, and "which contracts let the client leave early?"

To defend a line, give four things: the choice, the reason, the cost, and how you would test it. A weak answer names a tool before a reason, never mentions a case where retrieval is not needed, and says "it works" without saying how that was checked.
