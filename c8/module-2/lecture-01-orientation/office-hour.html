Office hour · Tue 22 Sep 2026 · After Lecture 1 · Both tracks

Tools change. The foundation stays.

How office hours run, one topic in depth (world models), the road ahead, and the room's questions, with a first look at the second brain.

Missed the office hour? This is what mattered from it, in about five minutes. It came three days after Lecture 1, led by the instructor who takes the no-code track.

01How these office hours run

The instructor opened by asking who had started First Proof, the brief from week 0. Several had, and one learner shared their start. Then he laid out the plan for every office hour.

  1. A weekly update. New releases and research from the week in AI.
  2. A progress check. Where you are with the current work.
  3. One topic in depth. About half an hour on where a field is heading and what it opens up for you.
  4. An open floor. Your questions on the Friday and Saturday lectures.

Why spend time on research news? Because what is in research today reaches the market in about three to six months. You hear it early, so you can plan around it.

Office hours are planned for Tuesdays and Thursdays at 8:30. The room asked for more discussion between learners, so the instructor will try sharing the topic a couple of days ahead.

02An LLM predicts the next word

This week's topic started from your phone. When you type a message, the keyboard offers three words that could come next. An LLM does the same job at a far larger scale. It has read a huge amount of text, and it predicts the next word.

Done at that scale, next-word prediction looks like intelligence. So where does it stop?

The instructor held up his phone. You know that if you press on it hard enough, it breaks. An LLM can say so too, because it has read the sentence. But it knows the answer without knowing the cause, so it writes well and still slips on actions in the real world.

03A world model predicts what happens next

Something that acts in the world needs a different prediction: if I do this, what happens? The slide showed a person, a table and a glass.

A system that learns this from watching the world carries a small simulation inside it. The simulation says how things behave, and how they change when you act. That is a world model.

One example was Genie, from Google. You type "a rainy Tokyo street at night" and walk into it. Underneath, it draws each next video frame from your key presses. Robots and self-driving cars are trained on the same idea.

Then the instructor asked: what if the simulation is wrong? A simulation is not reality. His verdict: world models are early, and they will add to what AI systems can do.

From the room: "I haven't got the actual concept of AGI"

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) has no single definition. Treat it as a yardstick that every company measures in its own way. Tests (hard exams, coding, using a computer) check how much of a person's work an AI can do. When an AI nears the top of those tests, people start using the word.

04The road ahead on the no-code track

Someone asked: "Will we be using any of these tools in our cohort work?" Maybe some, the instructor said. But every week brings a new one.

The curriculum is the foundation: a way of thinking that lets you pick up whatever arrives next.

Then he showed the order of the no-code track.

You start with design. Where design alone runs out, you add a backend with Antigravity, then a database, then a first working version (an MVP). The later boxes are names for now, and each gets its own lecture.

The track is built for people who know a line of work from the inside. What counts is how well you understand the problem. The instructor's own goal for the room: apps that earn real money.

05"How do you codify that domain knowledge?"

This question came from the room. A learner building an app for a field they know well wants to direct the AI, not do every step themselves. But the AI does not have what they know. How does that knowledge get in?

Lecture 1 met the same wall: the editors' judgment had no written form. The instructor's answer was a preview. You take what you actually understand and write it down as files an LLM can read: plain text files (Markdown files) and skills. The course calls that set of files a second brain.

Another learner had already started one: a notes folder with an inbox, a page about themselves and a folder per project. It worked, though their tools did not yet read from it every time.

The instructor said two things about it:

  • There is a structured way to do it. The method is how you collect your sources and arrange them. It gets its own lecture, about a month from this session, and stands on the lectures before it.
  • It keeps changing. A second brain on day one cannot be compared with the same one after you have collected more and given it structure.

06From the Q&A

  • "Could you include payment gateways?" Yes, briefly. It comes up when the room reaches a limit of Google AI Studio, and it is shown once for everyone.
  • "How will we learn how to get users for the things we build?" That comes closer to the build. It covers how to pitch and how to set up a sales funnel.
  • "Can I come to you with feedback on the lectures?" Yes. Use this measure: an office hour takes one hour of your weekday, and it should be worth that hour.
