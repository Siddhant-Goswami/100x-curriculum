Your sheet is the data

Why the Lecture 9 sheet is the same work AI labs now pay experts to do, and why the next step is writing the eval.

Missed the office hour? This is what mattered from it, in ten minutes. It assumes you have started the practice set: your atomic tasks, each with success criteria, a verifier and a verification type, and one row tested on the model.

01What a lab needs

Every AI lab spends on three things. Two are out of reach for most of us. One is not.

The labs say the open internet is largely used up (Ilya Sutskever, December 2024: "we've achieved peak data"), and the walled-off parts are being licensed (Reddit's deal with OpenAI is one). What is still scarce is expert judgment on real tasks: what a good answer is, and where the model's answer goes wrong.

That work has moved. A few years ago an expert wrote one prompt and one ideal answer. Models can now produce those themselves by searching. Where they still fail is long tasks: a lawyer working with a model for days, where it drifts five times along the way. Someone who knows the domain has to say where, and why.

02It is the sheet you are filling

Expert-work listings on data platforms such as Mercor read like this, for a lawyer: write a problem from a matter you worked on, with the facts and the reference answer; grade a model's draft against it; flag where the model states the law correctly and would still lose. The engineering listing is the same shape: grade model-written code, and flag where it passes review and would fail in production.

In Lecture 10 we did exactly this on one question, "Which track should I choose?": ideal input, ideal output, the model's output, and the difference naming the cause. Repeat that for one task, 100 or 200 times, and it becomes a golden dataset for that task.

03Which rows matter

  • Rule rows: same input, same output. Automate them with a script or a formula. No model needed, and no lab needs your help with them.
  • Judgment rows: this is where the value is. A rule cannot say whether the output is good; an expert can.
  • Unverifiable rows: leave them for now. They come back when we build agents.

So from your sheet, pick one or two Judgment rows that move your North Star. Those are the ones worth turning into data.

04Two ways to scale what you know

Either way, step one is the same: know the one narrow task, and how you would check it.

  1. Build your own. Fine-tune a small open model on one narrow, verifiable task. Coinbase reported doing this for fraud prevention and beating frontier models on that task at lower cost. Note the scope: one task, not "replace Claude". It takes money, data and patience, and few people finish. We fine-tune a first model later in the course.
  2. Partner first. Turn one or two Judgment tasks into a dataset and work with a lab or a data vendor. You learn the whole cycle, and get paid for it, before you build anything of your own.

Whichever you choose, nobody can do either without the sheet: the task, its success criteria, its verifier, and real examples.

05From the Q&A

  • "My work is research. How do I verify that?" "Research" is not one task; it is closer to a North Star. Break it into atomic tasks (estimating the market size, for example) and write a verifier for each. The method is the one from Assignment 1: observe, hypothesise, test.
  • "I'm not a domain expert." Ask why you were hired and not let go: the work that only you do well in your role. That is the expertise. Write those tasks down.
  • "Won't the labs stop needing experts?" No. The world keeps changing, and a model with no fresh data goes stale the way a genius from 50 years ago would be lost today. The bar for good work keeps rising too.
  • "Should I start collecting data now?" First talk to whoever would use it, and ask what shape they need. Data nobody asked for is effort with no buyer.

06Next: write the eval

Look at the score you gave your tested row in step 04. It was out of 10, and it was by feel. Give the same output to someone else and they might give it a 4 where you gave it a 7.

A lab cannot use a score like that, and neither can you. The grader in that listing is paid to apply a check that two people would apply the same way. Writing that check is the next step: the decision, the hard lines, what a 1 and a 5 look like, and who grades.

Write the eval for your tested row

On your own, about 30 minutes. It goes in a new tab of the same sheet, same submission.

Keep this

What a model still lacks is an expert's judgment on real tasks, written down: the task, what success means, how to check it, and real examples. That is your Lecture 9 sheet. The eval is what makes your judgment usable by anyone else.
