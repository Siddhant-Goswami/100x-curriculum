What to hand to an agent

An agent is fast, cheap and sometimes wrong. Here is how to decide what it does, and how far it runs alone, on a week of your own job.

    The defaults come from each task's wording in O*NET. You know your week better: set the minutes from your log and flip any answer. The map above follows.

    The framework on one card

    Ask four questions of every task, in order. Each one only matters once the one before it holds.

    1. 1Did you log it for a real week?If not, log first
    2. 2Could a junior sign it, can the agent reach the inputs, and can you check it in two minutes?If not, it stays with you
    3. 3Is a mistake easy to undo and kept away from clients, money and other people's numbers?If not, Accelerated at most
    4. 4Did it pass 8 of 10 on your own tasks in the last 60 days?If not, Accelerated for now
    5. Four yeses: it may run Autonomous. Expect new work for checking it.

    The colours are the map's. Amber is deterministic: a rule checks it. Violet is probabilistic: the output varies, so you judge each one. Green is human. Coral is what you do next.

    Zoom out: the week becomes a K

    Run the same four questions over every worker and the economy splits like a K: one path where the agent does your job, one where you run the agent. Which path you're on depends on the colours in your bundle, and on whether you hand work over or are the work being handed over.

    Second-order effects

    1. Checkable work gets cheap. Jason Wei's Verifier's law: "The ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is. All tasks that are possible to solve and easy to verify will be solved by AI." So anything you can check in two minutes heads toward the agent's price. Pay moves to what can't be checked: judgment, accountability, a name on the decision.
    2. The bottom rung goes first. Research briefs, meeting packs and first drafts are the amber pins, and they are how juniors used to learn judgment. Fewer people climb to the upper arm unless firms train them on purpose.
    3. Leverage compounds. The hours that come back go into green work, which earns more, which buys better agents. The gap widens every year, not once. Directing agents becomes a skill like managing people.
    4. New work appears on both arms, but not the same work. Up top: designing the workflow, scoring runs, signing the output. Below: checking and labelling someone else's runs, usually paid per task.
    5. Access picks the arm early. Muse is not sold in India, Dots needs a $200 plan, and every agent needs inputs that are digital and reachable. Those with the subscription, the connected tools and the English get leverage first.
    6. Trust becomes the scarce input. Blast radius can't be delegated. Whoever answers when a run goes wrong is the one clients pay, so being trusted to sign is worth more, not less.

    The arm is chosen task by task, not once. Hand over the amber pins, move the hours into green ones, and learn to direct agents before your own bundle turns amber.

    This is an argument, not a forecast. To see which work tasks are being augmented and which automated today, look them up in the Anthropic Economic Index, which uses the same O*NET tasks.

    Which agent, as of 18 Oct 2026

    The framework names no agent. This list does, and it dates fast. Drop any agent you cannot use from India or whose connectors miss the task's inputs. Then prefer a published hands-on test over press reports over the vendor's word. On a tie, take the smaller permissions, then the lower cost.
