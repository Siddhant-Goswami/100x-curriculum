Agent Delegation Framework

The masterclass asked one question: how do you move one layer down the AI funnel? The answer was not a new tool. It was a new way to look at your own work. Watch the recap, then run the same steps on your own job.

Where are you?

One to two billion people have tried a chatbot. About twenty million use AI for long, complex work. Roughly five thousand build with swarms of agents. Pick the layer that sounds like you today.

Step 1: list your tasks

List tasks from your actual job. Each one has to pass two tests. It repeats: you do it every day, or every other day. It matters: automating it would really pay off. Ordering groceries repeats, but it unlocks nothing. Daily competitor research, a weekly report, a queue of support tickets: those are worth a look.

Type it the way you would say it and press Enter. A close match only adds task suggestions.

    Step 2: can you verify it?

    This is the filter that decides everything. For each task, ask: can I verify it was done well? If the answer is no, keep it human for now: a coaching conversation is hard to score. If the answer is yes, write the verifier: the checklist a good result has to pass.

    A verifier, for example: competitor research for a headphone brand

    • Every rival covered
    • Live sources cited
    • What changed is flagged
    • Ends with a plan for today

    That checklist is what lets an agent work without you. The rule: if you can't verify a task, it won't get automated. It is the funnel's lesson again: AI is strongest where the work is verifiable.

    Write the verifier for each task

    An amber task can be checked by a rule a program could run. A violet one needs up to three criteria a judge can apply the same way every time. Jev tests each one; a violet task stays human until its criteria are clear.

    A job is a bundle of tasks

    Zoom out, and every job is a bundle of tasks. Anthropic's Economic Index breaks jobs down exactly this way. Take a nurse. Helping a patient bathe stays human. Drafting discharge notes and planning shifts get augmented. Ordering ward supplies can be automated, because the invoice shows if it was right. And a new task appears: checking the AI's work.

    Steps 4 and 5: measure, learn from failures

    Pick an agent, build the automation, then measure it. Two questions choose the agent for you: are you comfortable with code, and is your data allowed to leave your machine?

    Run it ten times and score every run against the verifier. Say five pass. The five failures are not waste. Each one is an input, a bad output and, once you fix it, a good output. That is training data.

    Tap a run to score it: pass, fail, or not run yet.

    Feed the fixed failures back, and fine-tune a model on your own task. Coinbase did exactly this: it tuned a nine billion parameter open model on its own fraud data, and beat frontier models at catching fraud, faster and cheaper.

      Why now: the fork

      The masterclass closed on a K-shaped economy. Not a dip and a bounce, but a fork. Down one branch, AI consumers: billions using what others build. Up the other, AI-native professionals, shaping the tools for their own work. And the gap keeps widening.

      What follows

      1. Checkable work gets cheap. Jason Wei's Verifier's law: "All tasks that are possible to solve and easy to verify will be solved by AI." Pay moves to what can't be checked: judgment, accountability, a name on the decision.
      2. The bottom rung goes first. Research briefs, meeting packs and first drafts are how juniors used to learn judgment. Fewer people climb unless firms train them on purpose.
      3. Leverage compounds. The hours that come back go into human work, which earns more, which buys better agents. The gap widens every year, not once.
      4. Trust becomes the scarce input. Whoever answers when a run goes wrong is the one clients pay, so being trusted to sign is worth more, not less.

      Sorting
      Each task goes to TypeSafe's Jev 1.13 (pinned to jev-1.13.0) with seven narrow questions: the three-bucket question asked twice with the options in opposite orders, then whether a fixed rule could check it, whether criteria could be written, whether it happens live, by hand or under a signature, whether an agent could reach the inputs, and whether a mistake could be undone. The page's code combines the answers; when they disagree or sit near the middle, it asks you instead of guessing. Each verifier you write goes back to Jev to test whether it is a rule, clear criteria, and about the task. On 300 labelled tasks from software engineers, founders, management consultants and IT services staff, the sort matches the label 96% of the time (macro-F1 0.96), keeps 92% of the tasks that should stay with you, and asks you a question on 3%; verifiers you write are judged right 97% of the time (19 Oct 2026). Jev reads literally and can be wrong: override any colour when you know better.
